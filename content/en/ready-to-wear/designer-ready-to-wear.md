Designer ready-to-wear means clothes designed by an identified designer, cut in standard sizes and produced in short runs. It sits between haute couture, which is made to measure and legally protected, and mass fashion, produced in very large quantities without a signature. What defines it is less a price point than a signature and manufacturing you can trace.

## What is ready-to-wear?

Ready-to-wear covers every garment sold finished, in standard sizes and produced in series. You buy it in a store and wear it the same day, with no fittings or alterations in a workroom.

The French equivalent, prêt-à-porter, appeared in the late 1940s as a direct translation of the American term. The Weill company is often credited with popularizing it in the early 1950s, though sources differ on the exact attribution.

## How is it different from haute couture?

The first difference is legal. **Haute couture has been a controlled designation since 1945.** Only houses approved by the French Ministry of Industry may use it, under criteria set with the Fédération de la Haute Couture et de la Mode. A haute couture piece is made to measure, largely by hand, for a specific client.

Ready-to-wear is not a protected term. Anyone can use it, from an independent label producing thirty copies of a dress to a global chain producing tens of thousands. That is why the word "designer" matters.

| | Haute couture | Designer ready-to-wear | Mass fashion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Designation** | Protected since 1945 | Unregulated | Unregulated |
| **Fit** | Made to measure | Standard sizes | Standard sizes |
| **Runs** | One-off pieces | Short runs | Very large runs |
| **Signature** | Approved house | Identified designer | Often anonymous |
| **Manufacturing** | House workroom, by hand | Known workshop, often stated | Multiple subcontractors |

## Where does designer ready-to-wear come from?

One date is the usual landmark. **On September 26, 1966, Yves Saint Laurent opened Saint Laurent Rive Gauche at 21 rue de Tournon**, in Paris's 6th arrondissement. It was the first time a major couturier opened a ready-to-wear boutique under his own name, outside his couture house. The gesture mattered because it showed that a designer's vision could live in standard sizes without giving up on standards.

Others had paved the way. **Chloé**, founded by Gaby Aghion in 1952, was built from the start on luxury ready-to-wear rather than couture.

In **1973** the industry organized itself. The **Chambre Syndicale du Prêt-à-Porter des Couturiers et des Créateurs de Mode** was founded on October 8, together with the federation that brought couture and ready-to-wear under one roof. Ready-to-wear shows then took their place on the Paris calendar alongside haute couture.

## How do you recognize designer ready-to-wear today?

Not by price, which varies enormously between designers. Rather by a cluster of signs:

- **An identifiable signature.** A cut, a fabric, a palette, a recurring detail. You recognize the piece before reading the label.
- **Short runs.** Designers produce in small quantities and restock sparingly, which is why a piece can sell out quickly.
- **Traceable manufacturing.** The workshop, country of manufacture and precise composition are stated.
- **Careful finishing.** Even seams, clean hems, a sewn lining, neat buttonholes.

## How to judge a piece before buying

A few simple checks work in a store or when an order arrives:

1. **Turn the piece inside out.** The inside tells you everything about how it was made. Cleanly finished seams, no loose threads, a properly set lining.
2. **Read the composition label.** A precisely named fabric (virgin wool, linen, silk, viscose) beats a vague one.
3. **Look for the country of manufacture.** Designers producing in France, Portugal or Italy usually say so.
4. **Ask.** A designer or a good store can tell you who made the piece and why it costs what it costs.
